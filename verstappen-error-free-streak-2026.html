219 Races Without Crashing Alone: The Full Verstappen Record

Zandvoort's lap-one wall ended the longest error-free run of Max Verstappen's career — a streak that began after Monaco 2016 and survived 27 retirements, none of them a solo mistake. We pulled the official record for every one of those races.

By F1wow Team 9 min read

ZANDVOORT, August 23 — When Max Verstappen dipped a wheel on the damp exit of the final banking and put his Red Bull into the wall seconds after lights out at his home Grand Prix, one number started travelling faster than the debris: 219. That is how many consecutive races the Dutchman had started since the last time he crashed out of a Grand Prix entirely on his own — a run stretching back to Monaco in May 2016, more than a decade ago. The graphic we posted after the race became the most-shared thing F1wow has ever made, and it came with careful fine print: excluding collisions with other cars, and excluding crashes caused by mechanical failures. A stat that big deserves to be stress-tested. So we pulled the official record for all 245 starts of Verstappen's career, checked every single retirement in the window, and cross-read the stewards' rulings where they exist. Here is the full ledger.

The Claim, Defined Precisely

The claim is not "Verstappen never crashes." It is narrower and more interesting: in the window between the 2016 Monaco Grand Prix and the 2026 Dutch Grand Prix, he never once retired from a race with a crash that was purely his own doing — no rival contact, no failed component, no tyre let-go. Collisions with other cars (Austria 2025, Italy 2021, Bahrain 2018) and crashes triggered by mechanical failures (Silverstone 2026, Bahrain 2017) are carved out, because they are not unforced errors.

That definition matters, because Verstappen does retire — 27 times across those 219 races, better than one DNF every eight starts. The question the stat answers is what kind of retirements they were. Strip away the failures and the accidents other people initiated, the graphic says, and you are left with an empty column — until Sunday.

The Last Time: Monaco, May 2016

The baseline is a weekend that could only happen to a teenager. Verstappen arrived at Monaco 2016 as Formula 1's youngest ever winner, having taken that shock Spain victory for Red Bull seven days earlier at 18 years old. Then Thursday practice happened, and so did the Swimming Pool chicane: a Q1 crash that wrecked the chassis and forced a rebuild, a pit-lane start, and, when the race came around in worsening rain on Sunday, a second shunt coming out of the tunnel on lap 34. The official status for the retirement reads, simply, Accident. He called it part of the learning curve at the time.

That day has an odd property: it is the hinge of Verstappen's entire career record. Every single thing he has done since — four world titles, 70 more wins, 130 more podiums — happened inside the error-free window it opened. Of his 71 career wins, only that first one in Spain 2016 falls outside the streak. The last time Max Verstappen crashed a Formula 1 car into a wall with nobody's help, he was an 18-year-old one-win wonder who had not yet led a championship. The next time, he was a 28-year-old four-time champion at his home race.

What Actually Ended His Races

Sort the 27 retirements by cause and the shape of the stat becomes clear. Fifteen were the car betraying him. Six were other drivers hitting him. Two were his own moves into another car — Monza 2021 and Baku 2018, the latter with teammate Ricciardo — which carry blame but not the word "unforced," because there was another car involved. Four carry an official status of Accident but with a documented extenuating cause. The bottom row is the one that had never been filled in.

The Asterisk File: Four Accidents Worth Arguing About

An honest audit has to open the four drawers marked Accident, because each one is a judgement call, and one of them is genuinely close.

Spa 2019 is the nearest the streak ever came to ending. On lap 1 Verstappen dove down the inside of Räikkönen at La Source, the cars touched, his steering was damaged, and he went straight into the wall at Raidillon. Alfa Romeo's team principal called it a "stupid" move; Verstappen's counter was that Räikkönen left him no room. There was contact with another car, so it fails the strict "crashed alone" test — but if you think the contact was a consequence of the lunge rather than the cause of the crash, you could score this one for the driver. It is the strongest counterexample in ten years, and the graphic's fine print is what keeps it out.

Singapore 2017 needs less debate: squeezed between both Ferraris metres off the line, the stewards examined it and filed it as a racing incident with no penalty. Sakhir 2020 was an evasive crash — Leclerc's late lunge at Pérez ahead of him left nowhere to go, and Leclerc was the one penalised. And Baku 2021 was not a driving error in any sense: a rear tyre let go at high speed while Verstappen led, and Pirelli's post-race investigation attributed it to an inner-sidewall failure linked to running pressures, explicitly not blaming the teams.

Add the two collisions that were his doing — Monza 2021, where he was penalised after his car ended up on top of Hamilton's Mercedes, and Baku 2018, where he and Ricciardo were both reprimanded after colliding as teammates — and you have the honest summary: Verstappen has caused crashes in the window. He has caused collisions. What he had not done, for 219 races, is lose a car on his own and put it in the wall without a single other variable involved. Zandvoort was the first.

Why 2026 Keeps Handing Him Excuses

The Zandvoort crash ends a streak that 2026 had already spent the year testing with failures rather than mistakes. In Shanghai, a power unit failure parked him with 20 laps to go, the weekend he told reporters the car was a "joke." In Monaco — of all places, the site of the baseline crash — his engine died at launch from the front row. At Silverstone he spun out of third place at Stowe with what the team traced to the same mechanical problem that had already spun him in Austria qualifying, a car that in his words "basically wasn't able to turn"; he was still classified 20th, so it does not even count as a retirement in the record.

Which makes the Dutch GP a clean break in a messy season: no mechanical asterisk available this time. The surface was damp in places, the tyres were cold, the exit of the final banking catches people — and none of it changes the classification. For the first time since he was 18, the cause column reads: driver.

The season now stands at 100 points from 12 rounds for Verstappen, winless since the streak was still alive, and the championship picture has moved on without him. The man who signed through 2030 a week earlier left his home race with a damaged car and a broken record — his own.

What the Streak Actually Measures

Ten years and 3,738 days without a self-inflicted crash is not the same as ten years without mistakes, and the streak's real meaning is stranger than "he stopped erring." Verstappen still retired 27 times in the window — once every 8.1 races. He still tangled with Hamilton in 2021, with Ricciardo in 2018, with Räikkönen's Alfa at Spa in 2019. What the number captures is a specific and remarkable kind of control: the complete disappearance of the solitary, no-one-to-blame, learning-curve shunt that defined his teenage seasons — Monaco 2016, and before it Monaco 2015, where he crashed chasing Grosjean in his rookie year.

The window also quietly contains most of his greatness: 70 wins and 130 podiums in 219 races mean he won 32 percent and podiumed 59 percent of the time while never once binning it alone. In 2023 he ran all 22 races without a single retirement of any kind. The streak finally snapped where it was always going to hurt most — at home, on lap 1, in front of the orange grandstands, at the circuit's farewell party.

The next race on the calendar is Monza. It is the track where the Monza 2021 collision happened, and it is where the new streak — currently zero — begins. Session details are on the Race Hub, and his full career numbers are on his driver page.

Methodology: How We Checked Every Race

Source data, definitions and edge cases — the full audit trail

Source. All 245 of Max Verstappen's Grand Prix starts were pulled from the official race record (Ergast/Jolpica motorsport database), including each entry's finishing classification, grid position, laps completed and official FIA status string. The streak window is the 219 races from the 2016 Canadian Grand Prix through the 2026 Hungarian Grand Prix — every start after his Monaco 2016 retirement and before the 2026 Dutch Grand Prix.

What counts as a retirement. Classifications of R, D, E, W or equivalent non-finish. One edge case: the 2026 British Grand Prix, where Verstappen stopped late with a mechanical problem but was still classified (20th, having completed the required distance share). It is therefore a classified finish in the record and is not counted among the 27 retirements — the dot matrix shows it as a classified result with a hover note.

What counts as an unforced error. A retirement where the official cause is a crash, no other car made contact with his, and no mechanical or tyre failure preceded the loss of control. Exactly two races in his career meet that definition after May 2016: none until Zandvoort 2026. Monaco 2016's retirement (a lap-34 crash in rain, after a pit-lane start following a qualifying chassis-breaking crash) is the baseline.

Verdicts. The "What the record shows" column reflects stewards' decisions and published investigations where they exist: the racing-incident rulings (Spain 2017 context, Singapore 2017, Bahrain 2018), Hamilton's 10-second penalty (Britain 2021), Antonelli's penalty (Austria 2025), the dual reprimands (Azerbaijan 2018) and Pirelli's Baku 2021 tyre-failure finding. Where no ruling exists, the official status string is reported as-is.

Grid position 0 in the data denotes a pit-lane start, as in Monaco 2016 after the chassis change.
